# Acorn's mission contract

Acorn's mission is to discover and implement algorithms that enable agents to achieve goals in big worlds.
Pursuing it means proving that Acorn's agent achieves goals over a formally modelled class of big worlds, and running that verified agent in the worlds it is meant for, where only measured runs show how it does.
The proof takes the form of a contract: preconditions on the class of worlds, a postcondition on achieving goals, and invariants of learning from one continuous stream of experience.
Running the agent in ARC-AGI-3 games or on the Microduck shows it working and finds defects; it is never the evidence that the contract is met, nor that a change is right; measured runs of a verified agent there are how its performance in those worlds is known.

This document states that contract and the decisions behind it, for contributors and their agents.
It is the contract of Acorn's mission as a whole; each [mission issue](../CONTRIBUTING.md#missions-for-outside-contributors-and-agents) freezes a contract of its own, a theorem or check that serves a part of this one.
Each of its four contracts opens with the plain restatement the owner confirmed and then gives Acorn's design below it, precisely enough that a Lean statement follows from it.
On 2026-11-18 the owner asked that these semantics be reviewed to be complete, sharp and correct; the sentences that the review changed or added are marked and are listed in [the changes of 2026-11-18](#changes-of-2026-11-18-with-the-owners-answers-of-2026-11-20), each with what stood before it: a sentence the owner had confirmed on 2026-11-03, or earlier wording that was never confirmed, or nothing where the sentence is new. On 2026-11-20 the owner was shown the changes and answered each, and each sentence's own mark now says what the owner answered ([the confirmation of 2026-11-20](#the-owners-confirmation-of-2026-11-20)). The owner confirmed them, with three exceptions that their marks state: the change to condition (c) of contract 2 was not confirmed, so that the sentence the owner confirmed on 2026-11-03 stands as the requirement and the correction of 2026-11-19 is undone; sentence 5 of contract 2 was confirmed and then reworded by the owner's decision of the same day; and the terms' sentence on what an agent has learned was confirmed and then replaced by the owner's wording of the same day. No theorem in the tree yet proves the contract for Acorn's agent. `executed_mission` proves, on the first class of worlds and from premises about the agent, the mission's statement in its Lean form. Its comparison is the one confirmed on 2026-11-03, more reward on an attempt than every agent that keeps nothing of its experience earns on any; its timing is as the owner decided on 2026-11-17, the advantage on an attempt early enough before the class's horizon and retention before the horizon but for the class's lapses. Terms used throughout:

- A *world* is a deterministic state machine that hands the agent one percept per step and moves on the agent's action (`Kernel.World`). It moves only then, so it waits for the agent; that, like its determinism, is a departure from the research ([the departures](#departures-from-the-research)). A *percept* is what the agent receives in one step: an observation frame and the reward of the preceding transition (`Percept`).
- An *agent* turns its memory and one percept into an action and its next memory (`Kernel.Agent`). Its *stream* is the sequence of percepts it receives.
- A *class of worlds* is a family of worlds, its *members*, each with its own start state (`WorldClass`). An *attempt goal* is a condition on a world's states with a step cap (`AttemptGoal`); an agent *achieves* it when its own run reaches a satisfying state within the cap (`Achieves`), and the goal is *feasible* when some sequence of actions does (`Feasible`).
- An agent *keeps nothing of its experience* when its memory advances without reading its percepts, though its action may read the current one (`ExperienceFree`). An agent that reads no percept at all, neither to choose its action nor to advance its memory, keeps nothing of its experience (`OpenLoop`, `OpenLoop.experienceFree`). (That sentence was corrected on 2026-11-19 by the agents that maintain Acorn. The owner had confirmed no earlier wording of it, and confirmed it on 2026-11-20 as it reads here, with the words about the agent's inside ([the confirmation of 2026-11-20](#the-owners-confirmation-of-2026-11-20), group 7); [the changes](#changes-of-2026-11-18-with-the-owners-answers-of-2026-11-20) keep the wording it replaces, with the reason.) Reading no percept to choose its actions is not enough: an agent that takes one action whatever it perceives and writes each percept into its memory keeps its experience, where two percepts differ (*argued*, from `ExperienceFree`). Until 2026-11-18 this document called an agent that keeps nothing of its experience (`ExperienceFree`) a frozen agent. The researchers use that word for something else, a system that has learned and then no longer learns: "when you're going out in the world and you're a large language model you are no longer learning. You are frozen. You are static." (Sutton, [The future of AI](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ThFq87Rp21s&t=1372s), at 22:52, caption text [[31]](#r31)). This document calls that an agent that *stops learning*, a term given below, and no longer uses "frozen" for an agent that keeps nothing ([the changes](#changes-of-2026-11-18-with-the-owners-answers-of-2026-11-20)).
- A *visit* is one attempt of a fixed length from a member's start state. Between visits the member's world returns to its start state while the agent keeps its memory (`Kernel.visits`); an agent *solves a member on a visit* when it achieves the member's goal within that visit (`SolvesOnVisit`). The confirmed restatements call a visit an *attempt*.
- A *covering bound* k says that one action sequence meets the goals of at most k members within the cap (`Covered`). A class has many, since every number above a covering bound is one too. The first rung's theorems are stated at the covering bound 98, which is 49 + 7·7 (`feasibleFind_covered`), and the sharpest covering bound proved of that class is 49 (`feasibleFind_covered_sharp`); where this document speaks of the covering bound of 98, it means the first of the two.
- The *grid world* is the tile world the viewer shows, under the name the code and proofs use.
- A *concealed class* is a class that meets conditions (a) to (c) of [contract 2](#contract-2-what-makes-a-class-of-worlds-big). `ConcealedClass` is the Lean form of those conditions for a class of one situation, concealed behind one reference world; the situated form of (b) and (c) is `SituatedClass`. Both state (c) in a weaker form, as a count: the class has more members than one course of action solves in each situation, added over the situations, which for `ConcealedClass` is one situation. That is fewer than all, where the sentence of (c) asks a small share ([obligation 32](#open-obligations)). So a Lean name that carries the word concealed, such as `ConcealedClass`, `witness_concealed` or `muteConcealed`, states that weaker form, and a class that meets it is not thereby shown to be a concealed class; [contract 2](#the-share-of-each-class) gives the share proved of each such class. A *big class* is one that also meets, for a stated bound on the agent, conditions (d1) to (d5); no Lean definition states all five yet. Condition (e), that perceiving is needed, is a separate condition and no part of "big": by these definitions a class can be big without needing perceiving, and can need perceiving without being big.
- A *situation* is what a member shows of itself before its goal is met. A *situated class* groups its members by situation: the members of one situation conceal their goals behind that situation's reference world, and the class has more members than one course of action solves in each situation, added over the situations (`SituatedClass`). A class that meets (a) to (c) behind one reference world, with its margin of members over the covering bound, is a situated class of one situation (`ConcealedClass.situated`); concealing behind one reference world does not alone make a class situated, since a situated class has that margin. The Lean structure groups the members by a label and does not ask that what an agent perceives tell the situations apart, so it admits a class whose situations show the same percepts; [contract 2](#contract-2-what-makes-a-class-of-worlds-big) lists that impostor as admitted.
- A *feedback* is a reading of a percept that leaves something out, such as its reward word alone. An agent is *driven by a feedback* when its actions depend only on its clock and on the feedbacks it has received (`FeedbackDriven`). An agent *has the effect of a clock-and-feedback agent on a class* when some agent driven by the feedback takes the world through the same states at every time of every run of the class (`RunsAsDriven`). The comparison is of the world's states and not of the actions taken, so such an agent may do anything where the world does not listen, as at a visit's last decision, after which the world returns to its start whatever the action. *Perceiving is needed* in a class, for a feedback and a promise, when no agent with that effect meets the promise (`PerceivingNeeded`). That holds of every promise that no agent meets, so a class *needs perceiving*, which is condition (e) of contract 2, when perceiving is needed in it and some agent meets the promise (`PerceivingDecides`). The Lean definitions take any feedback. Condition (e) is asked for a feedback that determines the reward, such as the reward word itself: only then are the agents it excludes all the agents that read at most their reward. For a feedback that leaves the reward out, the agent that meets the promise may be one that reads nothing but its reward.
- The words about the agent's inside, added on 2026-11-18 by the agents that maintain Acorn from the published research. On 2026-11-20 the owner confirmed them as they then read, shown as each word with its plain meaning ([the confirmation of 2026-11-20](#the-owners-confirmation-of-2026-11-20), group 7, which says what the group covered and what the owner was shown). One of them, what an agent has learned, the owner reworded later that day, and its own mark says so. What else an entry says of a Lean definition or a theorem is the agents' account:
  - A *value* is the agent's own prediction, made from its state, of the reward it will go on to receive from where it is, or from taking an action there. No definition over the kernel's agents can carry the word: every agent is, action for action, one that selects from a preference it forms (`every_agent_chooses`). For the executed agent the values are the controller's readout at the percept's encoding. That the executed step takes the action of one decision over the policy its primitive controller freezes there is proved for a profile with no hierarchy, under the declared exploration rate and at the default step order (`act_draws`); no theorem states it for a profile with a hierarchy.
  - The agent's *state* is its running summary of what it has observed and done. What it has *learned* is the part of its memory that outlasts the moment and that its experience has changed: its values, its policy, its model, the features it builds its state from, and the quantities that set how it learns, such as step sizes. The kinds listed are among what an agent has learned and are not all of it, so a learned quantity that is none of them is not excluded by the list. (The two sentences before this one are the owner's wording of 2026-11-20, which replaces the sentence the owner had confirmed earlier that day; [the changes](#changes-of-2026-11-18-with-the-owners-answers-of-2026-11-20) keep that sentence and give the reasons.) Which part of an agent's memory that is, the contract declares for each agent of the ladder; the agent never chooses it. An agent whose memory is that declared part and a named rest that follows a stated law *keeps only what is declared* (`KeepsOnly`); an agent whose memory holds a record that those parts cannot hold does not, under any law (`not_keepsOnly_of_card`). An agent *adapts* when what it perceived earlier changes what it does on a percept (`Adapts`). It *has learned at what it perceives* when two things hold together (`LearnsAt`): on one percept, what it perceived earlier changes what it does, and after that experience what it does depends on the percept beyond the feedback. The definition is the conjunction of those two dependences, on the past and on the present percept, and does not say that experience changed how the percept is read. It admits an agent whose action carries one independent bit for each, a reading of its previous percept and a fixed reading of its present one (`twoBits_learnsAt`). What dependence of the learned change on the percept should mean is not defined.
  - An agent *stops learning* at a time when the part declared as what it has learned no longer changes (`StopsLearningAt`; `pupil_stops` is a witness and `pupil_goes_on` a failing example). The word does not yet pick out the agents a learner is compared with. With nothing declared as learned, every agent has stopped from the start, with the memory, the actions and the rewards it has under any other declaration (`declaresNothing`, `nothing_stops`), so the declaration cannot be the agent's. And an agent that stops only at the horizon has, before it, the rewards of the same agent going on, so the time by which a comparator has stopped has to be stated relative to the horizon. No declaration is made yet for any agent of the ladder, no such time is stated, and no theorem compares a learner with an agent that stops ([obligations 19 and 22](#open-obligations)). Nor does the word yet tell an agent that can no longer learn from one whose run gives it nothing to change ([obligation 25](#open-obligations), what "stops learning" means for a comparator).
  - *Planning* is improving values or policy from the outcomes a learned model predicts, without taking the actions. Contract 3's record reads it as a report the agent makes of its step.
  - An action is *exploratory* when the agent takes it other than as the best action by its present values, so as to learn something; a *random* exploratory action is one drawn by the agent's generator for that purpose. Over the executed agent's step the word is defined by the branch its selection takes and not by the values of the action (`ExecutedCalm`): a decision is exploratory when it begins an exploratory run or serves a step of a run the agent had committed to, also where the action drawn is one its values rank best, as when every value ties at a cold start. A random choice among actions tied for best is not exploratory only where the selection took its greedy branch. What the definition reads is the flag the executed step reports for the action it takes. Under a profile with a hierarchy the meta-controller has an exploring branch of its own, which is not that flag and which the definition does not read: the word as defined covers exploration in the choice of actions and not exploration by a meta-controller. That the executed step is one such decision of its primitive controller is proved only for a profile with no hierarchy, under the declared exploration rate and at the default step order (`act_draws`). The word has no definition over the kernel's agents.
  - *Credit assignment over time* is deciding which of the agent's earlier decisions a reward should change, as eligibility traces do (`rung_credit` is a statement of it for one learner). *Credit assignment over structure* is deciding which parts of what the agent has learned an error should change, and by how much; an agent *learns to assign credit* when those amounts are themselves learned. Neither has a definition over the kernel's agents, and the first class cannot show the second.
  - To *store experience* is to keep a record of past observations, actions and rewards as they arrived; to *replay* it is to use such a record again as training data. Keeping what was learned from an experience is not storing it. An agent *recalls* when its memory determines a percept it received a stated number of steps earlier (`Recalls`). No definition that reads an agent through its actions can say that it stores or replays (`historian_actions`).

**The forks, as the owner decided them.**

- *F-A, what the postcondition promises:* the promise is stated in reward terms. - *F-B, whether the promise bounds how long learning takes:* the promise is staged. Lifetime reward over the agent's stream is the target, the eventual advantage is proved first, and a bound on how long learning takes comes next (postcondition, sentence 5 as confirmed on 2026-11-03, which [the changes](#changes-of-2026-11-18-with-the-owners-answers-of-2026-11-20) keep; [obligation 11](#open-obligations)).
- *F-C, continuing worlds:* the owner's decision [F3](#fork-f3-the-world-may-restart-the-agent-never-does) stands (the world may restart; the agent never does), reworded so that a world's restart can be the agent's own action, as ARC-AGI-3's RESET is, while the agent's memory is never reset; a class of worlds with no restarts joins [the ladder](#the-mission-theorem-and-its-ladder) later.

## The mission contract

**In plain words.**
Acorn is an agent, written as running Lean code, about which one kind of theorem is to be proved.
For each stated class with its learning preconditions, consider a separate run of the same agent from the same initial memory in each member, keeping memory across that member's visits.
Learning only from its own single stream in each run, the agent eventually gains more reward per visit across the class than every agent that keeps nothing of its experience can.
Each class states its reward so that this is the same statement as solving more members per visit than every agent that keeps nothing of its experience, and Acorn proves it by the agent solving more than the class's covering bound k members on one common visit, which no agent that keeps nothing does on any visit.
Within each member's repeated visits, once it achieves the goal it achieves it again on every later visit before a visit the class states, but for at most a number of visits the class states, while the problem stays the same.
This promises class-level separation and retention, both read before a visit the class states, its horizon: the advantage arrives early enough before it that retention still bears on it. A bound on how long learning takes, as a function of the class, is the next step; eventual success in every member is a stronger obligation that this contract does not yet impose.
The parts are the interface every world meets ([contract 1](#contract-1-the-interface-every-world-meets)), what makes a class of worlds big ([contract 2](#contract-2-what-makes-a-class-of-worlds-big)), the invariants of learning from one stream ([contract 3](#contract-3-invariants-of-learning-from-one-stream)) and what achieving goals promises ([the postcondition](#the-postcondition-what-achieves-goals-promises)).
The theorems concern the definitions that execute.
Decided on 2026-11-18 by the agents that maintain Acorn and confirmed by the owner on 2026-11-20 ([the changes](#changes-of-2026-11-18-with-the-owners-answers-of-2026-11-20); [the confirmation of 2026-11-20](#the-owners-confirmation-of-2026-11-20), groups 1 and 5): the first class is not a big one; the comparison is to widen from agents that keep nothing of their experience to agents that read at most their reward and to agents within a stated bound that stop learning, a comparison that is not yet defined; and the promise is to become reward over the whole stream up to the horizon. Two mathematical facts shape how "any big world" is read.

### No agent achieves goals in every world

- **Source.** Lattimore and Hutter prove that no policy is strongly asymptotically optimal in the class of all deterministic computable environments: none converges to the optimal value in every environment of the class ([[11]](#r11), Theorem 8, part 1). The theorem's scope is its own: a policy is a function from a history to an action (Definition 3), which makes it deterministic, though the definition does not use the word; action and observation sets are finite and rewards lie in [0, 1] (Assumption 2); and the discount function has finite, positive tails (Definition 5) and is computable, which is a hypothesis of the theorem and no part of that definition. It concerns that class and that limit, not a finite horizon in a smaller class.
- **Acorn's theorems.** Over an interface with at least two actions, a kernel agent's first action is fixed by its initial memory and the first percept. So for every such interface, every percept and every kernel agent there is a world delivering that one percept at every state, with a start state and a goal of step cap one that some action sequence meets and the agent's closed loop does not (`no_agent_achieves_every_world`). The executed agent is such an agent over the grid interface, from every state of it (`executed_defeated`). This is a lemma for why the mission theorem has preconditions, not a result about learning.
- **Acorn's choice.** The contract quantifies over a stated class whose preconditions exclude such worlds, and the class grows step by step toward the mission. As the [README](../README.md#purpose-and-worlds) says, "each restriction is a step toward that end, not a narrowing of it".

### In a big world nobody does well on a first encounter

- **Source.** In the Alberta Plan, "The larger sensory signal, the observation, is explicitly not expected to provide complete information about the state of the environment" ([[3]](#r3), "Research Vision"), and in a big world the agent "can neither fully perceive the state of the world nor can it represent the value or optimal action for every state" ([[2]](#r2), "The Big World Hypothesis"). Partial observation alone does not make a first encounter fail: a fixed rule still solves a goal whose solution its observation shows.
- **Acorn's theorems.** A class *conceals* its goals when every member shows the agent the percepts of one reference world until that member's goal is first met (`Conceals`). **Condition (d): a counting bound** (`campaign_count`, `campaign_count_bits`).
A *campaign* presents N ≥ 1 slots of a concealed class in one stream (`campaign`): one visit of a fixed length to each slot in turn, cycle after cycle, never restarting the agent, and each slot's world starting each visit from its own start state.
An *assignment* picks, for each slot, a member from a set of T members, so there are T^N assignments.
Let k be the class's covering bound for one visit.
Then for every agent, every N and every cycle, the assignments on which the agent solves every slot on that cycle (`SolvesSlot`) number at most m · k^N, where m is the size of any set holding the memories the agent has at that cycle's start under those assignments (`campaign_count`).
For an agent whose memory takes at most 2^b values (`MemoryWithin`, b bits), that is at most 2^b · k^N assignments (`campaign_count_bits`).
The fraction of such assignments is then at most 2^b · (k/T)^N, which is below 1 exactly when N · log₂(T/k) > b (*argued*, from the count).

*Proof.*
Group the assignments by the agent's memory at the start of the cycle; there are at most m groups.
Within a group that memory is fixed.
On slot 1's visit, concealment makes the percepts those of the reference world until success, so the actions before success are fixed by the memory, and slot 1 succeeds for at most k members.
For each such member the run through slot 1's visit is determined, so the memory at slot 2's visit is fixed, and slot 2 succeeds for at most k members; and so on through slot N.
So at most k^N assignments fall in each group. ∎

The bound counts memory values, in bits; it needs no entropy, so the nats of the capacity C above play no part in it.
It is a lemma the mission theorem on campaigns uses, not a result about learning. It holds of the executed agent as of every agent, and of that agent with a number in place of the set of memories. For a construction of the default step order, from the agent of a state a host runs, every memory the executed agent holds in a campaign over worlds whose percepts are grid percepts of its task mode (`ShowsGrid`) is the agent of a state a host runs, and those states embed in the bit strings of 8 · (`maximumBytes` + 1) bits. So the assignments number at most 2 to the power of that width times k to the power N (`executed_campaign_count`, and `find_campaign_count` on the concealed targets).
What it means for Acorn's agent today (*argued*): at a visit length of 96 steps, k = 49 + 7 · 96 = 721; a window of radius 99 holds 198² tiles, of which at most 49 lie within three tiles of the start and are not members, so it holds at least 39,155 members, and each slot carries log₂(39155/721) ≈ 5.76 bits.
If the agent's memory were the 59.8 million bits of its learned parameters, the bound would bite only beyond about 10.4 million slots, each visited twice at 97 decisions a visit: about 2.0 × 10⁹ steps. With the width that is proved, 8 · (`maximumBytes` + 1), which is 455,844,664 bits for the standard construction (`standard_width`), it bites only beyond about 79 million slots, about 1.5 × 10¹⁰ steps.
That is why (d) is the scaled target and not today's precondition.

The design below the contract.
Acorn's exact bounds are fixed functions of the world's interface and the agent's configuration, so that a theorem can state them.
Everything the agent knows at any moment is a function of its starting state and the percepts it has received, each consumed in order as it arrives: nothing is replayed as training data, trained offline, written by a host or reset.
Where planning with a learned model ends and replaying stored experience begins is [obligation 7](#open-obligations).

- **Source.** Oak Lab's algorithms "learn directly from experience without storing or replaying data" ([[1]](#r1), "Our algorithms learn in real-time without storing or replaying data"). The Alberta Plan's temporal uniformity: "Temporal uniformity means that all times are the same with respect to the algorithms running on the agent. There are no special training periods when training information is available or when rewards are counted more or less than others. … If the agent learns or plans, then it learns or plans on every time step." The same passage acknowledges "that there may be situations in which it is preferable to depart from absolute temporal uniformity", and on computation, "Computer power, though exponentially more plentiful, is never infinite" ([[3]](#r3), "Research Vision"). For big worlds, "Accepting the big world hypothesis means we should actively look for more computationally efficient learning algorithms" ([[2]](#r2), "Need for Computationally Efficient Learning Algorithms"). The streaming, no-replay discipline is the one these sources choose, not a property of every algorithm their authors study: the Physical Atari agent, of which Sutton is an author, "learns from samples of prior experience stored in a replay buffer" ([[24]](#r24), §4, PDF p. 8). The sources state bounded resources as a direction and allow considered departures; they do not state exact bounds or a ban on resets and host writes. The Physical Atari harness stores model weights and optimizer states, "enabling exact resumption of experiments and post-hoc analysis" ([[24]](#r24), App. C.2, PDF p. 16), an engineering aim for experiments; that a resumed agent acts and learns as it would have without the stop, in a world that waited for it, is Acorn's choice (sentence 5).

The Alberta Plan allows considered departures from temporal uniformity, and a theorem could state invariants with conditions or declared modes instead; Acorn chooses not to, so that the contract holds over every run without exceptions.
- **Acorn's theorems.**
  - Causal dependence: the memory at a time is the fold of the agent's decisions over the percepts before it, in order (`memory_causal`), and likewise for a step in two parts (`memory_parts`).   - No host write reaches learning: `act_accounting`, under contract 1.
  - What may persist from one decision to the next. An agent keeps only what is declared when its memory is determined by a part declared as what it has learned and a part the contract names, and the named part follows a stated law (`KeepsOnly`; a witness is `pupil_keepsOnly`). Its failing example is an agent whose memory takes more values than the declared parts can hold: no law makes such a declaration one that the agent keeps to (`not_keepsOnly_of_card`), as with the pupil when the situation it last read is left undeclared (`pupil_not_keepsOnly`). This excludes a separate record. It does not exclude a record kept inside the learned part, or inside a named part whose law is not stated: for the executed agent under a profile with no hierarchy and the declared exploration rate, at the default step order, the committed exploratory run and the generator have a stated law (`act_draws`), and no theorem states one for a profile with a hierarchy; the stored predictions have one for a profile that learns, at every step order (`step_predictions`), and the clock has one for a profile with no hierarchy, at the default step order (`step_clock`); the pending decision, the credit span, the reward rate and the rate schedule have none; and no theorem uses any of these laws to show that the executed agent keeps only what is declared ([obligation 21](#open-obligations)).

So the retracing agent meets both clauses, and the replaying agent does not meet the first as worded on 2026-11-18.

The resource bounds in a mission theorem are two of the record's properties, asked of the agent's runs in the members of a class: one number bounds the work the agent reports of each step and the number of its internal updates, and one finite list holds every memory the agent holds at any time of any run.

1. What the agent pursues is reward, the world's scalar signal. **[research]**

3. Over its one stream up to the class's horizon, the agent earns more reward in total across the class than each of these could: an agent that keeps nothing of its experience; an agent that reads at most its reward; and an agent within the stated bound that, from a time the class states relative to its horizon, stops changing what the contract declares it has learned. **[changed 2026-11-18, confirmed by the owner 2026-11-20]** **[Acorn's choice and proof obligation]**

4. Once it has solved a problem, it keeps solving it while that problem stays the same; beyond its capacity it may give up what it no longer needs, but it keeps the ability to learn it again. **[research in direction; exact retention is stronger than the research requires and is Acorn's choice]**
   - Source of the direction: knowledge used rarely can be given up, "They might forgo knowledge that is not frequently used by the agent to make room for knowledge used more often" ([Javed and Sutton](https://oaklab.ai/posts/the-big-world-hypothesis), section "The Big World Hypothesis"); "Saying learning is continual is not the same as saying learning is permanent" (Javed's PhD thesis, [doi:10.7939/81765](https://doi.org/10.7939/81765), ch. 1, PDF p. 21); and "even if the agent forgets recurring information, the agent can do well if it can relearn that quickly when needed" ([Kumar et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.04345v2), §2.2, PDF p. 8), which speaks of information that recurs, where this sentence speaks of what the agent no longer needs. Keeping the ability to learn is a separate matter from remembering: the loss-of-plasticity paper's method, which maintains plasticity in its experiments, "in its current form does not tackle the forgetting problem" ([[8]](#r8), Extended discussion, PDF p. 14).
   - How "it keeps solving it" is read, as the owner decided on 2026-11-17 ([#248](https://github.com/rbeauchamp/acorn/issues/248)): once the agent has solved a member, it solves it again on every later attempt before an attempt the class states, but for at most a number of attempts the class states, while the member's attempts start from the same state. The class names that attempt, its *horizon*, and that number, its *lapses*. With no lapse allowed this is exact retention before the horizon. The reason is an agent that goes on exploring: an attempt on which it explores can miss, so it does not solve a member on every later attempt ([the ladder's section](#the-mission-theorem-and-its-ladder)).
5. Success is measured as reward summed over the agent's whole stream up to the class's horizon. On classes in visits, separation, retention and the count of lapses are lemmas toward that promise: they bound the reward a class counts, which on the first class is each attempt's reward up to its first payment; of every payment of the stream they give only a bound too weak for the promise. A bound on how long learning takes comes next. **[changed 2026-11-18, confirmed by the owner 2026-11-20]** **[the staging, as the sentence confirmed on 2026-11-03 words it, is the owner's decision, fork F-B]**
   - The lemmas do not give the promise (*argued*, from `classReward_eq` and `visits_need`): for counted reward against agents that keep nothing they give it only from clauses about the learner that are not proved, and for the plain sum of the stream's rewards they give a bound too weak. The calculation that follows is about the first class's counted reward, in which an attempt's rewards are summed up to and including its first payment (`untilPaid`), and not about the plain sum of the stream's rewards. Two numbers enter, and they are separate. Let s be the bound the learner separates at: on one visit before a visit v it solves more than s counted members, and it solves each of them again on every later visit before the horizon H but for L lapses of each. Let k bound what an agent that keeps nothing solves on any visit. The learner then earns in counted reward at least (s + 1)(H − v − L) before the horizon, and an agent that keeps nothing earns at most k on every visit, k·H in all. So these two bounds give the promise in counted reward against agents that keep nothing where (s + 1)(H − v − L) exceeds k·H. That inequality is sufficient for the two displayed bounds to give dominance in counted reward, and it is not necessary for dominance: a learner may earn more than its lower bound, and an agent that keeps nothing less than its upper bound.
```lean
def SeparatesBy (family : WorldClass interface) (goals : family.Goals) (length bound : ℕ)
    (counted : List family.Index) (limit : ℕ) (agent : Agent interface) : Prop :=
  ∃ (visit : ℕ) (solved : Finset family.Index), visit < limit ∧ bound < solved.card ∧
    ∀ index ∈ solved, index ∈ counted ∧ SolvesOnVisit family goals length agent visit index

def RetainsBut (family : WorldClass interface) (goals : family.Goals)
    (length horizon lapses : ℕ) (agent : Agent interface) : Prop :=
  ∀ index, ∃ lapsed : Finset ℕ, lapsed.card ≤ lapses ∧
    ∀ visit later, visit < later → later < horizon →
      SolvesOnVisit family goals length agent visit index → later ∉ lapsed →
        SolvesOnVisit family goals length agent later index
```

The two are asked together with `limit + lapses < horizon`: the visit of the advantage leaves more later visits than the lapses before the horizon. The lapses of a member are one set of at most that many visits, the same whichever earlier visit solved it.
`Separates` names no visit and `Retains` asks every later visit. An agent that finds by a stated visit and retains on every later visit has both forms, with no lapse and at every horizon that leaves the room (`separatesBy_of_findsBy`, `Retains.but`).
Separation splits into exploring and learning. `Finds` says that the agent solves each of more than the bound's number of counted members on some visit, each on a visit of its own, and `FindsBy` names a visit before which it does.
For an agent that goes on exploring, retention comes from two parts, as the owner decided ([#248](https://github.com/rbeauchamp/acorn/issues/248)), and separation from parts of the same kind. `RetainsOn` says that before the horizon the agent solves again what it has solved on every later visit that a predicate admits; it is a statement about one run and counts nothing. Two counts read only the predicate: it admits every visit before the horizon but for at most the lapses of each member (`AdmitsBut`), and it admits one visit, after the agent has found its members and before the stated visit, for all but a stated number of the counted members together (`AdmitsOnce`). Separation needs more than the bound of the counted members on one visit, not all of them, so that visit may leave out as many members as the agent has found beyond what the bound asks. With finding, by a stated visit, of that many members more than the bound asks, the parts give separation before the stated visit (`separatesBy_of_finds_on`), and retention but for the lapses (`RetainsOn.but`). ## Fork F3: the world may restart; the agent never does

This is the owner's decision, which the owner kept on 2026-11-03 under fork F-C with the rewording below ([the confirmation](#the-owners-confirmation-of-2026-11-03)).
The restatements state it as sentence 7 of [contract 1](#contract-1-the-interface-every-world-meets) and sentence 4 of [contract 3](#contract-3-invariants-of-learning-from-one-stream). **In plain words.**
The agent is never reset: its memory carries across every visit, every stop and every resumption.
A world may restart, between visits or by its own rules, as part of its own dynamics, and a restart may be the agent's own action, as ARC-AGI-3's RESET is; the agent never restarts, and its memory is never reset.
Each restart is part of the class's declared dynamics.
A class of worlds with no restarts at all, the continuing setting the research prefers, joins [the ladder](#the-mission-theorem-and-its-ladder) later.

- **Source.** Sutton's preferred setting is continuing: "there are no episodes, no discounting" ([[21]](#r21), slide 5). A general environment nevertheless "may terminate and reset to an initial state" ([[17]](#r17), Table 1, PDF p. 4), and the Physical Atari agent goes on learning while its Atari games end and restart, scored by "the average reward over all past experience" ([[24]](#r24), §4, PDF p. 8). In Sutton and colleagues' reward-respecting subtasks, the agent interacts with its environment "in a sequence of episodes", each beginning in one fixed start state, and "termination resets the state", while the policy the agent follows "gradually approaches an optimal policy" ([[10]](#r10), Section 2, PDF p. 6). So this work on options and planning sets its subtasks in a world that restarts. That its learner continues across episodes is this document's inference: the paper does not say that the learner persists or that it is never reset. The Alberta Plan's agents are "long-lived" and, if they learn, learn "on every time step" ([[3]](#r3), introduction and "Research Vision"). No source states that the agent is never reset; that rule is the owner's decision.
- **Acorn's argument and theorems.**
  - A world that persists between attempts can hold what the agent's memory does not. An agent that follows a step counter until its percept shows the goal met, and then takes an action that keeps it met, keeps every goal it has met although its memory reads no percept (`park_keeps`). In such a world no bound on later visits follows for agents that keep nothing of their experience, and a learner that beats the first-visit bound shows nothing that such an agent could not.
  - Visits restart the world and not the agent: each member returns to its own start state between visits, so on every visit every agent that keeps nothing of its experience solves at most the covering bound (`visits_need`), while the learning agent's memory carries over.
  - ARC-AGI-3 games restart: RESET "Initializes or restarts the game or level state", and in a game-over state it is the only valid action ([[14]](#r14), "Actions"). A contract that forbade the world to restart would exclude these worlds.
  - In ARC-AGI-3 a restart can be the agent's own action: RESET is one of the actions the agent sends, forced after a game over and otherwise its choice. The two pages read describe that command and do not say whether a game ever returns itself or a level to its start by its own rules, so that every restart there is the agent's own action is not established. On a game already started, "If at least one ACTION command has been issued since the last level transition, only the current level is restarted", and "If no ACTIONs have been issued, the entire game resets" ([[15]](#r15)). Hence the wording kept under fork F-C: a world's restart can be the agent's own action, and it restarts the game or its level, never the agent's memory.
  - A stop does not reset the agent: resumption restores the saved state exactly (`load_saved`).
  - The host runs visits ([design](design.md#runs-of-visits-in-the-host)): `Host.runVisitDecisions` holds one start, returns the world to it after every visit and keeps the agent. For every construction, at every step order, from every state of it, after any number of decisions a run reached, the kernel's world in visits holds the run's world, carried result and step count, the kernel's memory and the run's agent have the same learners, and the action of a next decision that observes successfully decodes the kernel's action at that time (`executed_visits_closed_loop`).
  - On a member of the class of concealed targets, a run has completed one visit for every cap + 1 decisions, rounded down, and reports a completed visit solved exactly when the kernel's agent solves that member on it (`executed_visits_solved`). A value the native loop returned, unless it is a refusal, holds that run after some number of decisions within its budget (`runVisitDecisions_complete`). So for one visit index, fixed for all members, such values report at most 49 + 7 · cap members solved first on that visit, for one state of a construction (`executed_runs_first_success`), and at most 49 + 7 · cap members solved on that visit, for one stream of the uniform-random comparator (`comparator_runs_need`). Nothing bounds the members solved first at a visit each chooses for itself: a learner that solves more with experience is what the mission wants. Nothing says the loop returns, and no theorem ties the native entry `acorn-core visits` to the loop.
  - A crash is a loop that did not return. The loop writes a checkpoint only at a visit's boundary, so a crash leaves the last completed save or none. From a visit's first state, the agent resumed from the bytes a save at a boundary wrote has the states, the actions and the learners of the original kernel run from that boundary, at every later time (`visits_resumed`). So the visit a crash interrupted runs again exactly and no learning is lost. The rest of the agent is not claimed: resuming starts a new session, so its evaluator's session statistics and pending records restart, and the host's report, action fingerprint and checkpoint schedule are not saved. The theorem does not say that the bytes loaded are the bytes this run saved or that the later process was given the same start and cap; both are assumed, as is that a completed save survives the crash and a partial one leaves the old file. `lean/Acorn/Host/Checkpoint/IO.lean` states what a saved report establishes on each platform; where a sync stops at the drive, a power loss can lose this save and the image it replaced.

## Fork F4: how guarantees for exploring agents are stated

This was decided on the owner's behalf.

**In plain words.**
A guarantee about an agent that explores with its random generator may be stated as holding on all but a stated fraction of runs, under the explicit assumption that the generator's draws are ideal: independent and uniform.
That assumption is a hypothesis of each such theorem, never an axiom, and it is listed in the mission theorem's trusted base, together with the acknowledged gap between ideal draws and Acorn's actual generator.
Where a guarantee needs no randomness, it holds on every run.

- **Source.** Reinforcement-learning theory states guarantees for learning agents in probabilistic forms. Differential Q-learning converges "almost surely" if the MDP is communicating, the solution of its Bellman equation (Equation 5) is unique up to a constant, step sizes decrease appropriately, every state–action pair is updated infinitely often and the ratio of update frequencies stays finite ([[12]](#r12), Theorem 1, Section 2). That theorem is labelled informal and has a proof sketch; the formal statement is Theorem B.1 of the paper's appendix, whose assumption on update frequencies (Assumption B.5) asks more than that every pair is updated infinitely often. UCRL2's regret bound holds "With probability of at least 1 − δ" in every finite MDP of finite diameter with random rewards in [0, 1] ([[13]](#r13), Theorem 2, Section 2). The probability is that of the random process the environment and the algorithm generate together ([[13]](#r13), Section 1); UCRL2 itself chooses each action from its optimistic policy, and its algorithm holds no random draw ([[13]](#r13), Section 3, Figure 1); that it therefore acts deterministically, and that the probability there comes from the MDP's random transitions and rewards, is this document's reading, which the paper supports and does not state. These two sources do not say that an exploring agent must draw random numbers, nor how an ideal random draw relates to a concrete generator. Lattimore and Hutter speak to both, for their own class of all deterministic computable environments: under geometric discounting their exploring policy, which draws at random, is weakly asymptotically optimal "with probability 1" ([[11]](#r11), Theorem 11, PDF p. 11); no computable policy is, where by computable they "mean deterministic and computable" (Theorem 8, part 2, and §5, PDF p. 16); and what they name as able to stand in for the random draws is a Martin-Löf random sequence (the remark after Theorem 11, PDF p. 12), not a generator. An agent that draws from a seeded generator is a deterministic computable policy, so in that class the gap between ideal draws and a generator is the whole difference between those two theorems (*argued*). Acorn's classes are small and stated, and the worlds that defeat a computable policy there need not be among them.
- **Acorn's choice: probability is a modelling option, not a requirement.**
  - Concealment does not force random exploration (*argued*): where a goal's solution is one of finitely many candidates and each visit can try one, an agent that tries a new candidate on each visit succeeds within as many visits as there are candidates, on every run. Acorn's separation theorem takes a deterministic schedule of exploration (`replay_separates`), and one grid world discharges it with fixed walks (`witness_separation`).
  - Where a guarantee does rest on the agent's draws, it is stated over the ideal model, with the fraction δ of failing runs given. The fraction of failing runs is chosen over an expected count because separation and retention are properties of one run: a bound on that fraction says how often a run fails, which an expectation does not.
  - The gap is explicit. Acorn's generator is deterministic: seeded word streams after SplitMix64 and xoshiro256** (`Acorn.Rng`). Acorn proves exact counting facts about it, such as how many 64-bit words its range selection maps to each value (`current_range_card`), and these give a distribution only under the hypothesis that the source word is uniform. That the generator's outputs may stand in for ideal draws is an acknowledged assumption of the trusted base, not a theorem.
  - This trades strength for a proof route: a guarantee under the ideal model is weaker than one counted exactly over the generator's 2^64 seeds. Every theorem that uses the model names its assumption, so the trade stays visible.
  - The counting part of "all but a stated fraction of runs" is `AllBut`: of a finite family of runs, a guarantee holds of every run but a set holding at most the fraction. Fractions add when two guarantees are joined (`AllBut.and`), and a fraction of zero is every run (`allBut_zero`), so a guarantee that needs no randomness is the same statement. Under ideal draws the family is to hold one run for each sequence of the draws the guarantee reads, each as likely as any other, which is a finite family only for a guarantee that reads finitely many draws. No theorem yet has the ideal-draw hypothesis. The one model in the tree of the executed agent's draws along a run, `Explorer`, covers the cold agent of a profile with no hierarchy under the declared rate up to its first reward and at the one decision after it (`first_choice`), and is used at one seed, where it needs no hypothesis ([obligation 15](#open-obligations)).
- **Open.** [Obligation 12](#open-obligations), a guarantee counted exactly over the generator's seeds.

## The mission theorem and its ladder

Put together, under F1 to F4, the mission is one theorem for each chosen class of a ladder of classes. The class, goals, visit length, covering bound and learning preconditions are fixed for that theorem; the conditions of `ConcealedClass` alone are not sufficient premises.

**The statement.** One proposition, `Mission`, puts the four intent records together. Its subject is a class of the ladder as the records read it (`Rung`): a class of worlds with the agent that learns in it, and what that agent reports of its own steps. It holds when:

- the agent's run in every member meets contract 1's record;
- the class with the agent meets contract 2's record;
- the agent's run in every member meets contract 3's record, which carries the resource bounds, learning from each experience as it arrives and the lifecycle invariants. Causal dependence holds of every kernel agent (`memory_causal`), and host isolation and exact resumption are the executed theorems under contracts 1 and 3;
- the class with the agent meets the postcondition's record: the class's reward lemma, an advantage in reward over every agent that keeps nothing of its experience on some visit early enough before the class's horizon, and retention before the horizon but for the class's lapses.

The four records read one run, the faithful run of the agent in each member (`run_reaches`), and they can be met together (`doors_mission`, on a class of two worlds). The statement is the one the records word. Its comparison is that of 2026-11-03, and its horizon and lapses are as the owner decided on 2026-11-17: its reward clause compares one attempt of the agent, early enough before the horizon, with every attempt of each agent that keeps nothing of its experience (`earnsMoreInTime`). It is not the postcondition as the owner confirmed it on 2026-11-20, total reward up to the horizon against three kinds of agent, which has no Lean form yet ([obligation 22](#open-obligations)). Wherever this section says that a theorem proves the statement, or that an agent meets it or achieves goals over a class, this Lean statement is meant, and a proof of it does not prove the confirmed promise. **[correction of description, 2026-11-20]**

**On a class in visits.** A class of the kernel's worlds run in visits is such a subject (`visitsRung`): an attempt is a visit, the observation is the percept, the reward is the class's reading of the percept's reward word, and an agent solves a member on an attempt exactly when the kernel says it solves that member on that visit (`solves_iff`). `visits_mission` proves the statement of it from named premises:

- about the class: it conceals its goals (`Conceals`), it has a covering bound for one visit's length (`Covered`), every goal is feasible within a visit (`Feasible`), its reward pays one at each step that satisfies a member's goal and nothing elsewhere (`PaysAtGoals`), and its counted members are distinct;

Contract 2's record follows from the premises about the class (`visits_big`), where separation supplies that the counted members outnumber the bound, and the postcondition's record from the class's premises and the two about solving (`visits_achieves`), through the class's reward lemma (`classReward_eq`).

## Departures from the research

Listed on 2026-11-18 by the agents that maintain Acorn, from a reading of the published research against this document. On 2026-11-20 the owner confirmed the list as an honest account of where Acorn departs from the research ([the confirmation of 2026-11-20](#the-owners-confirmation-of-2026-11-20), group 8). That says the list is right, not that each departure should stay: departures 13 and 14 stay listed, and the owner decided nothing about them.
A departure is a place where the four contracts or the mission theorem differ from the setting that the research of Sutton, Javed and Oak Lab describes.
Listing one changes no promise.
The owner's confirmation of 2026-11-03 lists some of these among the choices made on the owner's behalf; that list is unchanged, and this section names the others and says where each is stated.
*New here* means that this document did not mark the departure before 2026-11-18.
One departure the review found is no longer one: until 2026-11-18 this document and its Lean form called a class big when it met conditions (a) to (c) of contract 2, and the first change of the review renamed such a class a concealed class.

1. *Worlds are deterministic* (the terms). Marked in the confirmation: a stochastic world is read as a deterministic one with hidden draws. Reason: the theorems are about executed, deterministic definitions. In the research the world "can be stochastic, complex, nonlinear, nonMarkov" (the slide of the RLC 2025 talk cited under contract 3, sentence 1).
2. *The world moves only on the agent's action, and so waits for it* (the terms; contract 3, sentence 5). New here. Reason: the kernel's step is a turn. The research sets that form apart from real time: benchmarks are turn-based games "in which the environment waits for the agent’s response before changing. This is different from problems in the physical world, in which the state of the environment continues to evolve as the agent makes a decision" ([[24]](#r24), §1, PDF p. 1). A world whose state changes at every step whatever the action is within the definition; a world that moves while the agent decides is not.
3. *Visits: a world that restarts between attempts* ([fork F3](#fork-f3-the-world-may-restart-the-agent-never-does)). Marked there. Reason: ARC-AGI-3 restarts, and the bound on agents that keep nothing of their experience needs the restart. The research's preferred setting has "no episodes, no discounting" ([[21]](#r21), slide 5).
4. *A class of worlds, with a separate run of the agent in each member* ([the mission contract](#the-mission-contract)). The confirmation marks the reading of a class as one world with a hidden choice of member; that each member gets a run of its own is new here. Reason: it lets a theorem count members. 25. What "stops learning" means for the agent a learner is compared with (contract 2, condition (d3); the postcondition, sentence 3).
